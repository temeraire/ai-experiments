## What the prism aftereffect is

It's the classic experiment from human psychology (Helmholtz, then Held & others) that we recreated in AB — and the experiment the project's flagship result is named for. In humans: put on prism goggles that shift everything you see, say, 30° to the left. At first you reach for a cup and miss by 30°. Over minutes of reaching, you adapt and hit accurately again. Then the goggles come off — and the crucial thing happens: **you now miss in the opposite direction.** That overshoot is the *negative aftereffect*, and it's the decisive evidence that your brain didn't just consciously compensate ("aim right") — it genuinely *recalibrated* the internal mapping between where things look and how you move toward them. The recalibrated map is baked in, so it has to be un-learned once the prism is gone, and during that window you err the other way.

Why it's decisive for us: a shallow "arousal / just approach the bright thing" account of AB's vision predicts *no* aftereffect — nothing was recalibrated, so removing the prism should just restore normal behavior. Only a genuine perception-action recalibration predicts the negative overshoot. AB showed it, on two seeds (s0 near−far +71, s2 +67), it's absent when the encoder is frozen or over-gentled (no recalibration → no aftereffect), which is why we say the aftereffect *tracks* recalibration.

## Governor vs. fine-tune vs. build-new — one axis, three points

These aren't three unrelated options; they're three points on a single axis of **how deeply the grounded signal is allowed to change the language model**, trading ceiling against cost and against forgetting:

**1. Governor (frozen LLM, external critic — architecture c).** AB's model sits outside a finished LLM and scores/vetoes/reweights its outputs. Cheap, reversible, interpretable, fast signal. But its ceiling is hard-bounded: *a governor can only reweight what the LLM already latently represents — it cannot install a representation the model never had.* And here's the rub the probe results expose: the meaning-cloud test showed the LLM's spatial vocabulary is phrasing-contingent and not stably grounded. If the geometry you want isn't reliably in there, a governor has little to grab onto. So the very evidence we have argues a pure governor will hit a **low ceiling** on exactly the spatial-meaning case we care about.

**2. Fine-tune an existing model (train a small adapter, or update LLM weights with grounded signal — architecture b, and its heavier RL cousin).** This is the pragmatic middle and, I'd argue, the actual sweet spot. The soft-prompt bridge (b) freezes the LLM and trains only a small projector from AB's encoder into the LLM's embedding space — cheap, and it *can* install new structure at the input without catastrophic forgetting. Heavier: use AB's model as a reward signal to fine-tune the LLM weights (RLHF-style, but reward = embodied-perceptual plausibility). That can genuinely install geometry the model lacked, at the cost of expense and forgetting risk. This is where "governor" and "fine-tune" actually blur — the soft-prompt bridge *is* a minimal fine-tune.

**3. Build a new model / our own embeddings (foundation — architecture d, + your atomization/physics-of-meaning ideas).** Grow language on the grounded substrate rather than bolt it on. Highest ceiling — it changes what representations exist at all — but §8's conditional bites hard: foundation only wins *to the extent the grounded substrate is broad*. Grow language on toward/away/red and you get a stunted speaker. The meaning-cloud result is the strongest current argument *for* this path (the phrasing-invariant meaning geometry lives in grounding, not in text embeddings, so you may have to build it), and your two captured ideas — atomization of meaning, physics of meaning — are the how. But it's the hardest build and the substrate isn't broad yet.

**My read on sequencing given the evidence:** the meaning-cloud result already tells us a pure governor (1) is probably ceiling-limited for spatial meaning, so I wouldn't over-invest there — use it only as the cheap de-risk to confirm grounded structure carries *any* actionable signal. The **highest-value next move is the adapter bridge (2)**: it's the direct A/B test of the whole thesis, it's cheap, and it's the same first step whether you ultimately aim at fine-tune or foundation. Build-new (3) is the theory-faithful north star but is gated on both a positive bridge result *and* a broader substrate — which is the direct incentive to keep enriching AB's perception. In short: **governor to sniff, adapter to test, foundation to aim at** — and the evidence so far tilts the eventual endpoint toward build-new, because that's where the phrasing-invariant meaning actually lives.

One caveat worth flagging: "our own embeddings" (foundation) and "fine-tune an existing model" can also be **composed** — §8 already names the likely real winner as a *hybrid*: text-pretrained breadth with a grounded core grown/fine-tuned on the substrate. It's not strictly either/or.

## What is mocap?

"Mocap" here is short for **motion-capture body** — a MuJoCo feature, not motion-capture in the film-industry sense. In MuJoCo, a body flagged `mocap="true"` is **kinematic**: it has a position and orientation you set *directly* from code every step, and physics does not act on it. It has no mass, feels no gravity, exerts no forces, and — the key property for us — with `contype=0 conaffinity=0` it **generates no collisions**. It's a puppet you move by hand; the physics engine just draws it where you put it.

That's exactly why it's the right tool for the prism ghost. We need to show AB a red ball at a *displaced* bearing (what the prism does to its vision) without that fake ball bumping into anything or being touchable. So the env:

Good question — I've been sloppy about grounding it. The degrees are **angles of direction**, measured from where AB is looking.

Think of AB with "straight ahead" as 0°. A ball off to one side is at some angle from straight ahead — say the ball is 40° to the right. That angle is the ball's **bearing**: 0° = dead ahead, positive = one side, negative = the other. Everything AB has to do — see the ball, turn toward it, crawl to it — is about getting that bearing right.

**The prism offset (the "30°") is how far sideways the lens shifts where the ball *appears*.** With a 30° prism, a ball that's really straight ahead (0°) *looks like* it's 30° off to the side. AB, steering by what it sees, heads 30° wrong and misses — until it re-learns. So "30°" is the *strength of the distortion*: a moderate sideways shove of the whole visual world. "60°" is a stronger one — twice as far off.

A clock face makes it concrete: if straight ahead is 12 o'clock, each hour mark is 30°. So a **30° shift is one o'clock's worth** — the ball looks one clock-hour to the side of where it is. **60° is two hours** (out to 2 o'clock). And the "±75° spawn cone" I mentioned just means the ball can start anywhere from roughly 10 o'clock to 2 o'clock in front of AB.

The other degree-numbers flying around are the same unit, used for error instead of distortion:
- "the eye's aiming error is 40–59°" = on average the eye's guess of the ball's direction is off by about that many degrees (badly blurry — 40° is more than a clock-hour of error).
- "signed bias +47°" = the eye is *systematically* off to one side by 47°, on top of the random blur.
- "residual slope 0.13 vs 0.64" = as we crank the prism from 0° up to 60°, does the eye's error grow with it (slope near 1 = it's fooled, tracking the shifted image) or stay flat (slope near 0 = it sees through the shift = recalibrated). The adapted eye's near-flat 0.13 is why that reads as recalibration.

So when I say "at 30° the ghost and the real ball are only ~0.4 m apart," that's because a one-o'clock shift at the ball's distance (~0.7 m away) moves its apparent spot only about 40 cm sideways — close enough that heading for the shifted image still lands you on the real ball, which is exactly why the 60° (two-o'clock) test is the clean one: there the two finally pull far enough apart to tell what AB is really steering toward.

Building on the last explanation — this is the trick that let us tell "the eye really recalibrated" from "the eye just learned to read the shifted image." It has two parts.

**Correlation vs. residual — two ways to grade the eye's guess.**

Say the eye guesses the ball's direction and we compare it to the truth.

- **Correlation** asks: *when the ball moves left, does the eye's guess also move left?* It measures whether they swing together — but it's completely **blind to a constant offset**. Picture a compass that's stuck reading 30° east of true north. As you turn, it still turns correctly and always points "the same general way" as reality — so it *correlates* perfectly with true north, even though it's wrong by 30° at every single position. Correlation can't see that stuck 30°.

- **Residual** asks the blunter question: *how many degrees off is the guess, actually?* The stuck compass has a residual of 30° everywhere. Residual **does** see the constant offset.

That's the whole reason correlation was useless to us here: an eye that reads the *real* ball and an eye that reads the *ghost* (which is always the-ball-plus-30°) both swing left-and-right in lockstep with the ball, so they have identical correlation. The 30° difference between them is exactly the kind of constant offset correlation is blind to. You have to look at the residual to catch it.

**"Across a sweep of lens strengths" — why one measurement isn't enough.**

A single residual number is still murky, because our eye has junk in it (a fixed ~47° miscalibration from the camera mount) that we can't cleanly separate from the prism's effect at just one lens setting. So instead of testing at one prism strength, we test at *several* — 0°, 15°, 30°, 45°, 60° — and watch how the residual **changes** as the lens gets stronger. That change is the giveaway:

- If the eye is **fooled** (reading the ghost): a stronger lens shoves the ghost further from the real ball, so the eye's error from the true ball **grows** as we crank the lens up. Error climbs with lens strength — a steep slope.
- If the eye **recalibrated** (sees through the lens): no matter how strong the lens, it still lands on the true ball, so its error from the truth **stays flat** as we crank the lens up. A near-zero slope.

That slope is the clean verdict, and it's what the numbers gave us: the un-adapted base had slope **0.64** (error grows almost as fast as the lens — it's largely reading the ghost), while the Stage-2 eye had slope **0.13** (nearly flat — its error barely grows as the lens strengthens, meaning it's reading the true ball regardless of the distortion). That near-flat slope is the fingerprint of genuine recalibration — and, crucially, it's a fingerprint correlation *literally cannot* show, which is why the monitor made me switch to the residual.
