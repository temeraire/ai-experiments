Now the honest answer to "is this a good idea?" — **partly yes, with one correction and one caveat that together decide whether it works.**

**The correction: AB is not actually starved for ball positions.** "Millions of steps" is steps, not episodes — an episode is up to 1000 steps, so millions of steps is already *thousands* of distinct episodes, each with a randomly drawn ball position. The current run you saw draws from `--spawn-cone-deg 136 --spawn-radius 0.70 0.80`, so every episode already samples a random spot in a 136°-wide cone. Random uniform sampling of a continuous space generalizes as well as — usually better than — a fixed grid, because a grid can alias and random covers the space in expectation. So "move the ball 1° at a time" across the field of view would **not** beat the random azimuth coverage AB already gets. Finer resolution along an axis it already randomizes buys little.

Where your intuition is genuinely right and *under-exploited* is the **under-sampled axes**, not resolution:
- **Distance.** The radius band is 0.70–0.80 m — a 10 cm shell. AB has essentially never trained across a wide distance range. Your "move it 1 cm at a time in distance" is real augmentation of the training distribution, and it's the exact lever behind the Phase V distance-law result (which extrapolated impressively but from a very thin band). This part is worth doing.
- **Shape, size, motion, clutter.** One object, mostly one shape, static-ish, one background. The impoverishment is in *variety across dimensions*, not resolution within one.

**The caveat that makes or breaks it: dense coverage alone can't prove generalization, and can actively hide memorization.** If you give AB every position, it can in principle build a dense lookup table over positions and show *zero* underlying generalization — and you'd never detect it, because you'd be testing where you trained. The rigorous form of your idea is already in our plan (the "holed product space" experiment, transcript line 1704): cover densely **but leave a deliberate gap, and evaluate on the gap.** Fill-the-hole = it learned the law; can't-fill-it = it memorized. Densification without a held-out region gives you the *illusion* of generalization for free.

**The honest limit:** this will sharpen and extend **proprio** generalization — which already works — and is a clean, cheap way to map its boundary. It is unlikely to fix the **vision** inertness. Our record attributes that to a structural cause (on the cart AB was a passenger, so there was no directional action for vision to *drive*, hence no gradient to encode direction) — not to sparse position sampling. More coverage of a signal the policy isn't using doesn't create usage. Coverage extends a capability that exists; it doesn't install a missing one.

One more thing hiding in your idea: the *continuity* part — small smooth changes between consecutive situations, the way a baby's world actually moves — has a real home that random teleporting doesn't capture. A slowly **moving** ball within an episode (continuous tracking) is a richer, more developmentally faithful signal than snapping to a new random spot each reset, and we already have the moving-ball machinery for it.

So my recommendation: yes, but redirect it. Don't spend the density on azimuth (already covered). Spend it on **widening the distance band and adding shape/size variety**, always paired with a **held-out region you evaluate on**, and consider a slow within-episode drift for the continuity signal. That's the version that produces generalization instead of a lookup table — and it's cheap (a spawn-range and curriculum-binning change, no new machinery).

**literature-scout — the *method* is textbook; we cite, we don't claim.** "Widen the range, punch a gap, test the middle" has standard names in four fields: it's *interpolation vs. extrapolation over a context set* (Kirk 2022; Procgen), and the exact rule-vs-lookup logic was published in **function-learning** (DeLosh, McDaniel & Busemeyer 1997 — extrapolation *is* the diagnostic that separates a rule from memorized pairs). The genuinely open space is not the design — it's the *substrate*: this design applied to an embodied, self-produced proprioceptive/visuomotor reach law. **local-book-agent — Taylor predicts the *shape* of the result.** Unexpectedly, our own shelf is nearly complete on this. ## The crux: do we have to imitate the baby?

**No — and we already have a written principle that says so.** It's in the glossary as *"faithful to the mechanism, free on the implementation."* The rule is: reproduce the load-bearing *mechanism* of how perception grounds — self-produced movement, motor competence established before vision is trusted, vision tied to action through cross-modal error — but treat the *incidental details of being a mammal* (gestation, helpless emergence, the literal year of crawling) as things to drop or compress. Your instinct is that principle talking back to you.

And here's the sharpest version of your own point: **our north star — prism/reversing-spectacle adaptation — is an adult phenomenon.** Stratton, Kohler, and Taylor ran those experiments on competent adults who already had a calibrated visuomotor system and then *re-calibrated* it under a perturbation. That is fundamentally different from an infant *building* the system from scratch. Recalibration needs a **competent baseline to perturb**, not a replay of infancy. So the reversing-spectacles goal actively argues for giving AB adult-like competence *sooner*, not for making it crawl through a simulated babyhood first.

There's one thing we genuinely can't shortcut, and it's worth naming so we don't throw the baby out with the bathwater: the grounding has to come from AB's **own** self-produced movement. We can't hand it a pre-calibrated map from outside — that would be ungrounded, which defeats the entire thesis. But "self-produced movement" does **not** mean "a helpless infant's movement." A competent, self-directed mover that produces its own motion and touches the world satisfies the mechanism completely.

And your discomfort with the sliding-on-the-platform, can't-steer setup is not just aesthetic — **it matches a bug we already diagnosed.** The single most important finding in the vision arc (R49) was that on the cart, *AB was a passenger* — there was no directional action for vision to drive, so vision stayed inert. Self-directed locomotion is precisely what finally made vision load-bearing. So "the baby can't manipulate where it's going" isn't faithful realism we should preserve; it's the thing that was holding us back.

**My recommendation on the crux:** stop treating the infant developmental path as a constraint. Keep only the mechanism (self-directed movement drives vision via cross-modal error), and get AB to *competent, steerable mobility* as fast as we can — because that's the on-ramp to the recalibration test, which is the actual goal.

## Parallax: it's already in the input — the question is whether AB *uses* it

Here's the slightly surprising part: **binocular parallax is present right now.** AB has two eye cameras (`left_eye` + `right_eye`), and the policy is fed *both* — `stereo=True` emits the stereo pixels into the observation (that's part of why the observation is 6213-dimensional). And because the head/body moves, *motion* parallax is physically in the pixel stream too. So we don't "introduce" parallax — it's there.

What we've **never done** is verify AB actually exploits it. That matters, because the distance law we just measured might be **proprioceptive** (AB knowing where its own body is) rather than visual — the ball-direction decode from the visual latent was near zero (R²≈0.08), which is a hint vision isn't carrying much depth. So the real "parallax" task is a **measurement**, and it's cheap because it needs no body change:

- **Mono-vs-stereo ablation** on a vision-only distance task: zero one eye and see if distance judgment degrades. If it does, AB is using binocular disparity. If not, the distance law is proprioceptive and vision isn't doing depth.
- **Static-vs-moving ablation** for motion parallax similarly.

That's a natural, sharp follow-on to the distance-generalization work — arguably one of the best cheap experiments we have, because it answers "is AB's depth genuinely *visual* and world-coupled?", which is close to the heart of the grounding thesis. So: **parallax comes soon, as a measurement riding on what we just built** — not a new thing to add.

## Grasping: this is a genuine new capability, and it belongs later

Grasping is a real jump, and right now we don't have it:

- The body is the **mitten-hand** MIMo model with **passive** fingers/wrists — the hands don't close.
- "Success" is currently **contact with any body part** (foot, elbow, torso, hand — `_check_ball_contact` fires on any geom), not a grasp. We even have a standing finding that the two-hand version of the task is essentially never completed (`both_HAND=0`).

So introducing grasping means three additions at once: **actuated hands/fingers**, a **grasp-detection success criterion** (closure around the object, not just touch), and a **reward** that makes grasping worthwhile — plus keeping it winnable (the grasp has to be physically achievable). That's a body-and-task change, so it belongs in the **richer-movement / bigger-body arc**, not now, and it's *not* on the critical path to the prism-recalibration north star.

But grasping has outsized *long-term* value, and it's worth saying why: it's one of the core **sensorimotor primitives that the grounding-to-language bridge is built on** — "grasp an idea," "a firm grip on the problem." If the eventual goal is AB's grounded map acting as a governor on an LLM, grasp is exactly the kind of image-schema primitive that abstract language is claimed to be metaphorically extended from. So grasping is the natural *centerpiece* of the manipulation/enrichment phase — the marquee item of Arc 2, once we've banked the recalibration payoff.

Fitting these into the plan from before:

1. **Stage-2 prism recalibration** (the north star) — now/next.
2. **Parallax ablation** — cheap, no body change, sharpens "is depth visual or proprioceptive?"; can run alongside or right after the recalibration work. This is the "when do we introduce parallax" answer: **soon, as a measurement.**
3. **DOF curriculum → grasping** — the richer-movement generalization arc, later, with grasping as its headline. This is the "when do we introduce grasping" answer: **after the recalibration payoff, as the core of the manipulation phase.**
